Introduction

A City Under Pressure

In Hong Kong's high-performance urban culture, the body has become another arena for achievement, anxiety, and consumer aspiration.

The rapid commercialisation of male sexual health since the 1990s intersects uniquely with Hong Kong's hybrid identity: a Chinese society shaped by Confucian masculinity norms, yet immersed in Western consumer culture.[4]

This project examines how pharmaceutical marketing, social media, and prevailing ideals of masculinity combine to create, amplify, and commercially exploit male sexual insecurity in the HKSAR.

Historical Context

A Brief History of Potency

From ancient Chinese medicine to billion-dollar pharmaceuticals — the commercial exploitation of male sexual anxiety is older than you might think.

~3000 BCE

Ancient Chinese Medicine

The Huangdi Neijing documents herbal treatments for male sexual insufficiency. Ginseng, deer antler, and rhinoceros horn prescribed — practices still visible in Hong Kong's TCM markets today.[5]

1870s

Victorian "Nerve Tonics"

Western patent medicines promised to restore male vitality. The medicalisation of male sexuality — and its link to moral character — becomes entrenched in consumer culture.[6]

1992

PDE5 Inhibitors Discovered

Pfizer researchers studying angina accidentally discover sildenafil's effect on erection during clinical trials. The compound increases cGMP, allowing smooth muscle relaxation.[7]

1998

Viagra Launches

FDA approves sildenafil. Over 1 million prescriptions sold within 3 months. US$1 billion generated in year one — the fastest pharmaceutical launch in history at that time.[8]

Landmark moment
2000

Arrival in Hong Kong

Viagra approved for HK sale. Counterfeit versions flood Sham Shui Po and Mong Kok markets simultaneously. By 2002, an estimated 30% of PDE5 inhibitors sold in HK are counterfeit.[9]

2003–2010

Competition Arrives

Tadalafil (Cialis) and vardenafil (Levitra) enter the market. Tadalafil's 36-hour window earns it "the weekend pill." Global ED drug market exceeds US$4 billion annually by 2010.[10]

2017–Now

Social Media & the Wellness Pivot

Marketing reframes treatment as lifestyle. LIHKG threads, WeChat ads, and Instagram influencers normalise recreational use among men under 35 without clinical ED.* [12]

Enhancement Culture

Selling the Ideal Man

How pharmaceutical companies and supplement brands turned a medical treatment into an aspirational lifestyle — and what this means for Hong Kong men.

Hong Kong's Unique Context

Confucian face culture meets Western consumer capitalism — creating unusually potent conditions for the commercialisation of male insecurity.

From Treatment to Lifestyle

Early Viagra advertising carefully framed the drug medically. By the mid-2000s, Cialis shifted to lifestyle imagery: couples at sunset, active men, taglines about spontaneity. The product was no longer treating a condition — it was selling an identity.[13]

Connell's (1995) concept of hegemonic masculinity explains the appeal: advertising constructs an idealised masculine subject who is sexually dominant, always-ready, and in control. Products are sold as access keys to this idealised state.[14]

The TCM Factor

Hong Kong's deep integration of Traditional Chinese Medicine creates a layered market where TCM supplements and pharmaceutical drugs co-exist and actively compete for the same anxieties. Brands invoke thousand-year-old formulas to build trust while targeting modern insecurities.[16]

Sexual performance becomes just another domain of masculine achievement — alongside career and financial status.

Prestige Positioning

Premium pricing and sleek packaging signal the product is for successful men. Pfizer and Eli Lilly use aspirational imagery mirroring luxury goods — suggesting sexual performance is a marker of elite status.

TCM Legitimacy

HK supplement brands invoke Traditional Chinese Medicine credentials. References to thousand-year formulas and Chinese medical authority figures build local trust while targeting the same modern insecurities.

Social Media Stealth

Due to prescription medication ad restrictions, brands use indirect influencer marketing, "wellness" framing, and forum seeding on LIHKG and HKDiscuss to reach consumers outside regulated advertising channels.

The Psychological Gap

Masters and Johnson's (1970) concept of spectatoring — where anxious self-observation disrupts performance — explains how expectation gaps become self-fulfilling. Mild difficulty triggers anxiety, which causes dysfunction, which confirms the feared inadequacy.[24]

In Hong Kong's context, research on "face" (面子) culture adds another dimension: sexual performance failures are experienced not merely as personal disappointment but as threats to social identity and relational status.[25] The pill becomes a way to defend one's standing, not just one's body.

Critical Analysis

Empowerment or Exploitation?

Do male potency drugs liberate men from physiological anxiety — or do they deepen the very insecurities they claim to resolve?

The Empowerment Case

  • For men with genuine clinical ED, PDE5 inhibitors are clinically proven treatments that substantially improve quality of life and relationship satisfaction[26]
  • Destigmatising male sexual health may reduce shame and encourage help-seeking in a culture where men historically avoid medical consultation[27]
  • Reduced sexual anxiety may yield secondary benefits: improved self-esteem, reduced relationship conflict, better mental health outcomes[28]
  • Some scholars argue consumer agency matters: men choosing enhancement products are exercising bodily autonomy, not merely submitting to social pressure (Williams & Bendelow, 1998)[29]

The Exploitation Case

  • Pharmaceutical companies profit by medicalising normal physiological variation. Expansion of "ED" diagnostic criteria has been criticised as disease-mongering (Moynihan & Mintzes, 2010)[30]
  • Marketing that frames sexual difficulty as personal failure reinforces toxic masculinity norms and prevents addressing root psychological causes (Tiefer, 1994)[31]
  • Recreational use among younger men without clinical indication may create dependency and cardiovascular risks from undiagnosed underlying conditions[32]
  • Hong Kong's grey market exposes consumers to significant risks from contaminated, misdosed, or counterfeit products[33]

Consumer Culture Theory & Masculinity Studies

Baudrillard's (1998) theory holds that modern consumption is fundamentally about the purchase of signs and identities. When a man buys sildenafil, he is not merely purchasing a pharmacological effect — he is purchasing membership in a category of men who are potent, in control, and sexually successful.[34]

The critical question, as Loe (2004) argues in The Rise of Viagra, is not whether these drugs work pharmacologically — they do — but what broader cultural work they perform: normalising an ideal of male sexuality that is mechanical, performance-oriented, and resistant to vulnerability.[36]

The cure for male sexual anxiety is not a better drug. It is a better idea of what men are for.

— Paraphrase of Leonore Tiefer (2004), Sex Is Not a Natural Act
Conclusion

What This Tells Us

Key Findings

1

40% of HK men 30–70 are affected by ED, yet stigma prevents most from seeking clinical help.

2

The market exploits and intensifies the gap between socially constructed expectations and physiological reality.

3

Hong Kong's hybrid identity — Confucian face-consciousness layered with Western consumer culture — creates unusually potent conditions for commercialised insecurity.

Broader Implications

The proliferation of potency-enhancing products represents a technological "fix" that displaces the more difficult work of renegotiating what masculinity means in a society characterised by gender role disruption and economic anxiety.

The critical question for Hong Kong is not simply whether these drugs are safe — most are, when used correctly — but whether a society that equates male worth with sexual performance is one worth reproducing.
