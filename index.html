Introduction

A City Under Pressure

In Hong Kong's high-performance urban culture, the body has become another arena for achievement, anxiety, and consumer aspiration.

The rapid commercialisation of male sexual health since the 1990s intersects perfectly with Hong Kong's hybrid identity: a Chinese society shaped by Confucian masculinity norms, yet immersed in Western consumer culture.[4]

This project examines the intersectionality of marketing, social media, and prevailing ideals of masculinity combined to create, amplify, and commercially exploit male sexual insecurity in Hong Kong.

Historical Context

History of Potency Drugs in HK & Development Timeline

From ancient Chinese medical philosophy to billion-dollar pharmaceuticals — the commercial exploitation of male sexual anxiety has a longer history than most people realise.

Ginseng — used in TCM for sexual vitality for millennia
Sildenafil (Viagra) — FDA approved 1998, transforming ED treatment globally
Pre-Modern

Ancient Chinese Medicine

Traditional Chinese medical texts such as the Huangdi Neijing conceptualise sexual health as part of overall vitality and balance. Sexual function is understood through broader ideas of life energy and systemic wellbeing, rather than as an isolated physiological dysfunction.[5]

19th Century

Victorian Era

Male sexual performance is increasingly interpreted through moral and medical lenses. "Nerve tonics" reflect broader anxieties about masculinity, degeneration, and self-control rather than targeted physiological interventions.[6]

Early 20th C.

Psychological Interpretation Era

Erectile dysfunction is widely understood through psychoanalytic and psychogenic frameworks. Freud's psychoanalytic theory significantly influenced the interpretation that sexual dysfunction stems from anxiety and unconscious conflict rather than biological causes.[7]

1990s

Biomedical Mechanism Discovery

Sildenafil is originally developed for cardiovascular conditions, then found to act as a PDE5 inhibitor. This mechanism increases cGMP levels, leading to smooth muscle relaxation and improved erectile function — marking a shift toward a clear biochemical understanding of erectile physiology.[8]

1998

Viagra Commercialisation

Following clinical approval, sildenafil (Viagra) rapidly became the first widely successful oral treatment for ED. Its introduction transformed ED from a largely private issue into a widely treated, openly discussed medical condition.[9]

Landmark moment
2000s

Market Expansion & Competition

Tadalafil (Cialis) and vardenafil (Levitra) enter the market, expanding options and intensifying competition. The global ED drug market experiences significant growth driven by increasing awareness, ageing populations, and broader acceptance of treatment for sexual dysfunction.[10]

2000s HK

Hong Kong Counterfeit Market

Counterfeit PDE5 inhibitors emerge within informal and unregulated markets in Hong Kong, raising serious public health concerns. Regulatory authorities highlight the risks of unregistered sexual enhancement drugs circulating outside formal medical channels.[11]

Regulatory concern
2010s–Now

Contemporary Period

PDE5 inhibitors are increasingly used not only for clinical ED but also in non-prescription contexts among younger populations. This reflects broader trends in digital culture, online pharmacy accessibility, and changing attitudes toward sexual performance and enhancement globally.[12]

Enhancement Culture

Selling the Ideal Man

How pharmaceutical companies and supplement brands turned a medical treatment into an aspirational lifestyle product — and what this means for Hong Kong men.

From Treatment to Lifestyle

Early Viagra ads framed the drug medically. By the mid-2000s, Cialis had shifted to lifestyle imagery. The product was no longer just treating a condition; it was selling an identity.

The Pharmaceutical Shift

Early Viagra advertising presented the drug in a serious medical way, helping distance it from older, disreputable sex stimulants. This made sexual performance treatment seem clinically respectable.[13] At the same time, consumer demand played a significant role — it was not only driven by prescriptions.[14]

This reflected a wider industry shift toward profitable "lifestyle drugs" aimed not only at people with medical conditions, but at the general public. The product was no longer just about treating a problem; it was selling confidence, normality, and a certain idea of male sexual identity.[15]

Connell's (1995) concept of hegemonic masculinity explains the appeal: advertising constructs an idealised masculine subject — sexually dominant, always-ready, and in control. Products are sold as access keys to this idealised state.[16]

食色，性也

shí sè xìng yě — "The desires for food and sex are part of human nature." — Mencius, 4th century BCE

In Chinese culture, sex has long been treated as something natural. But natural does not always mean easy to talk about. For a long time, sexuality in modern Chinese society was surrounded by silence and restraint — a tension in which sexuality mattered, but remained difficult to discuss directly.[17]

From the late 1980s onward, sex became more visible in Hong Kong public life through newspaper ads, magazines, and products aimed at male sexual performance. Sexual problems were no longer only private concerns; they increasingly appeared in advertising and everyday consumer culture.[17]

Sexual Performance & Masculinity

Male sexual ability is often linked to broader ideas of masculinity. Men are expected to be confident, capable, and able to satisfy their partner. Failing to perform can feel like failing to live up to what a man is supposed to be.[18] The ideal of the "perfect male lover" creates pressure — leaving little space for insecurity or uncertainty.

Enhancement Culture & Products

For many men, sexual enhancement products are not just about function — they offer reassurance and a way to protect self-worth. If sexual ability is tied to masculinity, then improving performance can feel like preserving identity. This explains why herbal tonics and potency drugs are marketed not only as solutions, but as ways to become "closer to the ideal."

Social Media Stealth

Due to prescription medication advertising restrictions, brands use indirect influencer marketing, "wellness" framing, and forum seeding on LIHKG and HKDiscuss to reach consumers outside regulated channels. Enhancement culture now operates largely through digital spaces where regulation is harder to enforce.

Sexual performance becomes just another domain of masculine achievement — alongside career and financial status.

Prevalence in Hong Kong

In Hong Kong, men encounter enhancement products in both medical and everyday settings. One study found that 50.9% of heterosexual men reported at least one sexual problem in the past year, while 42.5% of Chinese men who have sex with men experienced sexual dysfunction.[19,20] According to the University of Hong Kong Faculty of Medicine, erectile dysfunction affects around 60% of men aged 50–60 and 70% of men aged 60–70.[32]

These numbers help explain why enhancement products are widely promoted through clinics, pharmacies, health columns, and online culture — marketed not only as treatments, but as tools for confidence, intimacy, and masculine self-worth.

The Reality Check

Waldinger et al.'s multinational study found a median IELT of 5.4 minutes — far below the 20–30 minute standard often implied by pornography and popular media. This does not mean shorter or longer experiences are automatically abnormal; it shows that media expectations often distort ordinary bodily variation.[21]

A population-based Hong Kong study of 1,506 men aged 26–70 found an overall ED prevalence of 36.7%, with prevalence increasing substantially with age.[1] Erectile difficulty is not rare or shameful, but a common health experience shaped by age, stress, work patterns, and psychological pressure.

Veale et al.'s systematic review of up to 15,521 men reported a mean erect penile length of 13.12 cm across professionally measured samples, yet many men overestimate what is average because their reference points come from pornography and commercial enhancement advertising.[22] The market does not simply respond to insecurity — it helps define the standards that produce insecurity.

For Hong Kong specifically, the issue is intensified by face culture, work pressure, and reluctance to discuss sexual health openly. When sexual difficulty is interpreted as a loss of masculinity rather than a normal health issue, men may avoid doctors and instead turn to unregistered enhancement products. Hong Kong's Department of Health has warned that unregistered sexual health products may contain undeclared sildenafil or tadalafil, which can cause serious side effects and interact dangerously with other medications.[23]

The reality is not that men are "failing." Many men are comparing themselves against unrealistic, commercialised, and medically misleading standards. The performance gap is not only biological — it is cultural.

Critical Analysis

Empowerment, Exploitation, or Both?

Male potency drugs should not be understood as simply good or bad. Their meaning depends on context.

The Empowerment Case

  • For men with clinically diagnosed ED, PDE5 inhibitors such as sildenafil and tadalafil can be legitimate medical treatments. The American Urological Association states that men with ED should be informed about FDA-approved oral PDE5 inhibitors as a treatment option.[24]
  • These drugs can reduce distress, support intimate relationships, and encourage men to treat sexual health as a valid medical concern rather than a private shame
  • Destigmatising male sexual health may reduce shame and encourage help-seeking in a culture where men historically avoid medical consultation[25]
  • Some scholars argue consumer agency matters: men who choose enhancement products may be exercising bodily autonomy alongside medical need (Williams & Bendelow, 1998)[26]

The Exploitation Case

  • The same products become problematic when marketed as lifestyle enhancers. The drug no longer merely treats dysfunction — it constructs a narrow model of masculinity based on constant readiness, control, and stamina
  • When ordinary bodily variation — tiredness, anxiety, ageing — is reframed as personal failure, commercial actors profit from insecurity. This is medicalisation: normal experiences converted into purchasable problems (Moynihan & Mintzes, 2010)[27]
  • Marketing that frames sexual difficulty as personal failure reinforces harmful masculinity norms and prevents addressing root psychological causes (Tiefer, 1994)[28]
  • Hong Kong's grey market exposes consumers to serious risk: unregistered products may contain undeclared sildenafil or tadalafil, and sildenafil is contraindicated with nitrates, risking dangerous blood-pressure drops[23]

Consumer Culture Theory & Hong Kong Context

Baudrillard's (1998) theory holds that modern consumption is fundamentally about purchasing signs and identities.[29] When a man buys sildenafil, he is not merely purchasing a pharmacological effect — he is purchasing membership in a category of men who are potent, in control, and sexually successful. The pill becomes a symbol of self-management in a city already shaped by competition, long working hours, and pressure to perform.

Hong Kong adds a distinctive dimension. Because sexual difficulty may threaten "face," some men may prefer discreet self-medication over professional consultation. This makes online or grey-market products attractive, but also increases health risk — reinforcing the cycle of insecurity the industry depends on.

Male potency drugs are medically valuable but culturally ambivalent. They are empowering when they help men address genuine health concerns with proper medical guidance. They become exploitative when commercial culture uses them to intensify shame, exaggerate "normal" performance, and turn masculinity into a problem that must be continuously purchased and upgraded.

Key Findings

1

36.7% of HK men aged 26–70 are affected by ED, yet stigma prevents many from seeking clinical help, driving them toward unregulated products.

2

The market exploits and intensifies the gap between socially constructed expectations and physiological reality, profiting from insecurity it helps create.

3

A better public-health response would combine accurate sexual education, safer access to clinical advice, stronger regulation of unregistered products, and broader cultural acceptance of vulnerability in men.
